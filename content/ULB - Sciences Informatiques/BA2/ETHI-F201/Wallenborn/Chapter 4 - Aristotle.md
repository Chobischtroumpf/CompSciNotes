This chapter focuses on the immense influence of Aristotle (~384 - ~322 BCE), whose ideas were admired and adopted for 2000 years. Aristotle founded the Lyceum (~335 BCE) and pursued a universal, empirical form of knowledge, relying on observations but not experimentation. He produced comprehensive syntheses across logic, metaphysics, biology, cosmology, physics, politics, ethics, and psychology. His methods varied by domain, including deduction, observation, definition, reviewing common opinions, and considering exceptions. He was keen on classification (simple bodies, living beings with vegetative, animal, and human souls) and incorporated Parmenides' principle of the excluded middle in his logic.

Aristotle's Cosmology conceptualized the cosmos as an organized, hierarchical world fundamentally separated into two regions, linked to his theory of elements and natural movement.
- The Sublunar World (Earth): Spherical, immobile at the center, characterized by change and corruption, composed of four elements (earth, water, air, fire). Movements are either rectilinear (natural, towards center or upwards) or violent. This world is likened to Heraclitus's world of flux.
- The Supralunar World (Heavens): Spherical, full, immutable, composed of a fifth element, the quintessence (ether), and characterized by perfect circular movements. This world is akin to Parmenides' world of permanence. Difficulties arose in explaining the junction between these regions and how celestial bodies emitted light and heat.

Aristotle's concept of the Four Causes is central to his philosophy, asserting that knowing a thing means understanding its cause or "why". Building on Presocratic ideas of material, formal, and efficient causes, Aristotle introduced the final cause (telos) as the most important. For example, the material cause of a chair is wood, the formal cause is the idea of a chair, the efficient cause is the carpenter, and the final cause is its purpose. This emphasis on purpose (teleology) is an anthropomorphic aspect of his philosophy, suggesting nature itself has a goal and strives towards its good. He conceived of a finite and eternal world with a "prime mover" (later interpreted as a distant God).

In his physics of movement, Aristotle elucidated the "being-in-movement" of the sublunar world, recognizing that being is simultaneously one (in act) and multiple (in potency) to allow for movement.
- Phusis (nature) is understood in opposition to tekhnè (mastery). Natural phenomena have their principle (arkhê) within themselves, unlike art, which has its principle in a human. Nature is seen as ingenious, organizing, fabricating, and healing.
- Movements are either natural (e.g., a heavy body falling towards its natural place, the center of the Earth) or forced/violent (requiring an external motor to persist). He believed heavier bodies fall faster and that forced movements are inversely proportional to the resistance of the medium.
- Aristotle posited no void, arguing that matter is continuous, movement requires force, natural movement has no single direction, and violent movement would be infinitely fast in a vacuum.

Aristotle's physics of elements aimed to explain alterations and substantial changes. He described alteration as a change in quality (e.g., tanning) and substantial change as the coming into existence of a new substance (e.g., birth of an animal, metal melting). His theory combined four elements (fire, air, water, earth) with four fundamental qualities (hot, cold, dry, moist). These elements combine to form "mixtures" where the constituent elements exist potentially, not actually, to prevent their dissolution by moving to their natural places.

Aristotle's Biology constitutes about 30% of his work and was unparalleled until the 16th century. It includes "History of Animals," "Parts of Animals," and "Generation of Animals," likely the result of teamwork. He considered the study of animals a special case of physics, describing over 500 species.
- His systematics involved defining species and classifying animals into groups like Enaima (red-blooded, e.g., mammals, fish, birds) and Anaima (lacking red blood, e.g., cephalopods, crustaceans, insects).
- He posited a plural soul (vegetative, sensitive, intellective) and emphasized the continuum of nature, with corals and sponges seen as intermediates between mineral, vegetal, and animal life. This concept was interpreted in the Middle Ages as an "échelle de la nature" (scale of nature), ranging from inanimate beings to humans, which remained influential until the 19th century.
- His biology was functional, with meticulous anatomical observations explained teleologically (organs' functions).
- In the generation of animals, he stated the male as the efficient cause and the female as the material cause, with the purpose of perpetuating the species. He also accepted spontaneous generation for some species, believing worms, insects, and even some frogs and mice could arise from the decomposition of earth, mud, manure, or other decaying matter, imbued with "psychic heat" from solar warmth.

Theophrastus (~371 - ~288 BCE), Aristotle's successor, is known as the founder of botany as a distinct discipline. His works, Causes of Plants and History of Plants, were major contributions in antiquity and the Middle Ages, treating plant morphology, classification, environmental influence, reproduction, and utility. He created specific botanical vocabulary. Theophrastus focused less on formal causes and sometimes adopted a "mechanistic" approach, using analogies between natural and artificial processes and the Aristotelian "efficient cause". He believed nature was subject to regular laws and, while not rejecting spontaneous generation, called for more precise investigations. He also recognized the role of sex in plant reproduction, a concept later lost. His book On Stones was the first known work on mineralogy, classifying stones by physical properties, not magical or therapeutic ones. Dioscorides' Materia Medica (1st CE) is mentioned as a later influential plant compilation.

The chapter concludes by reflecting on the inquiry on nature in this period:
- The debate between final causes (Plato, Aristotle) and non-final causes (Empedocles, atomists).
- The value of observation (Aristotle vs. Plato).
- The enrichment of philosophical vocabulary (substance, quality, matter, cause).
- The application of mathematics and empirical research.
- The limited use of experimentation by the Greeks, as their problems did not require it, and they lacked sufficiently precise theories.
- The predominantly oral transmission of knowledge and the small number of individuals dedicated to science, due to a lack of financial incentives and the absence of the modern idea that science leads to material progress.

Finally, a comparison is made between Greek (Aristotelian 4 elements, purification) and Chinese (5 elements, transformation) ontologies, noting the cyclical nature of Chinese elements.
