This chapter delves into the philosophy of science, posing fundamental questions: "What is science?", "What history of science?", and "How do sciences evolve?". It explores whether science can be defined by a unique method, noting that the definition of science has evolved and is a subject of debate in epistemology. The "demarcation problem" – distinguishing science from non-science – is highlighted, with Kant's work as an early example. Different approaches to scientific methodology are mentioned: purely deductive (Aristotle, mathematics, logic) and inductive (Francis Bacon, based on observation/experience, but limited by David Hume's critique). The OHERIC (Observation, Hypothesis, Experiment, Results, Interpretation, Conclusion) model is presented as a pedagogical simplification.

The chapter then introduces three influential figures in 20th-century philosophy of science:

1. Karl Popper (1902-1994): He sought a demarcation criterion to exclude non-sciences like psychoanalysis and Marxism. He proposed "falsificationism" (or refutability), stating that scientific theories are characterized by their possibility of being disproven by facts. One cannot verify a theory completely, but one can refute it. The more a theory takes a "risk," the greater its success if verified. However, objections to Popper include that scientific "facts" are theory-laden and their recognition is debatable. His criterion would exclude fields like cosmology, history, and climate models, which often accommodate observations. Other criticisms are that he prioritizes theories over experiments, that theories develop ad hoc arguments, that falsifying observations can be wrong, and that historically important theories often emerge "falsified" but are not immediately abandoned.
2. Thomas Kuhn (1922-1996): He introduced the concepts of "paradigm" (shared examples defining problems) and "disciplinary matrix" (rules, theories, practices, instruments). His approach is more sociological, focusing on how scientific communities structure themselves around these concepts. In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962), he described science's evolution as discontinuous, progressing through stages: normal science, emergence of anomalies that the current paradigm cannot explain, and scientific revolution, leading to a new paradigm (a "conversion"). Kuhn argued against a continuous accumulation of knowledge, emphasizing "ruptures". He believed scientists don't critically challenge their own paradigms, assuming solutions exist within it. He also argued against the neutrality or objectivity of the lone scientist, viewing knowledge as a provisional consensus within a community. Limitations of Kuhn include interpreting "paradigm" as a full world representation rather than an articulation of data, and a form of relativism due to "incommensurability" between theories.

3. Paul Feyerabend (1924-1994): He famously stated, "anything goes!" as the only universal definition of science, highlighting the absurdity of seeking a single demarcation criterion. He argued against immutable methodological rules that stifle scientific imagination, suggesting that "anarchism" is necessary for knowledge development. For Feyerabend, values and ideologies are essential to knowledge, and there's no inherent superiority of science over myths; this must be demonstrated case-by-case, and often science has been imposed by colonizers. He advocated for the separation of science and state, and believed that rational knowledge progresses through discussion, making heterodox viewpoints valuable for challenging existing knowledge.

The chapter also covers the sociology of sciences, which focuses on scientific institutions, proof regimes, researchers' concrete work, community structure, and guiding norms.
- Laboratory studies (from the 1970s) analyze the technical content and knowledge production within labs, showing how "facts are made"—actively fabricated to be robust and reproducible. This reflects a constructivist view.
- Analysis of controversies reveals that science is full of debates (discussions on unresolved questions). When a controversy is ongoing, arguments fly, and actors show their biases. Bruno Latour's principle of generalized symmetry suggests that explanations from society should be given equal weight to those from nature, and one should not explain how a controversy closed by invoking the "truth" or "nature" retrospectively. Scientific statements gain validity not by being simply "true" or "false," but by their ability to mobilize and connect a network of allies (actor-network theory). Claiming "Science said so, therefore it's true" is risky.

The production of scientific facts relies on rigor (describing data collection and processing) and peer review, a quasi-universal procedure. However, published articles are not definitive proof, as scientific literature contains abusive interpretations and is a place of ongoing debate. Controversies can take decades to resolve and may resurface with new advances, underscoring that hypotheses can always be questioned.

A list of criteria of scientificity is provided, noting that not all need to be met simultaneously: clarity, predictability, universality, repeatability/reproducibility, objectivity (exclusion of bias), simplicity, falsifiability/refutability, fecundity (explanatory power), and internal/external coherence.

The conclusion of this section emphasizes that there is no one scientific method, but a plurality of methods. It distinguishes between "lab sciences" (physics, chemistry) and "field sciences" (geology, evolution, ecology, archaeology) which use different methods. The roles of theory and experimentation vary across disciplines, and theory doesn't always precede experience. Human and social sciences (SHS) can be rigorous but face challenges due to competing theories and the inherent instability of the human as an object of study.

The chapter also discusses diverse histories of sciences:
- The "history – discovery" (a 19th-century myth of the detached, individual genius) vs. the principle of symmetry (avoiding hindsight bias).
- The "invention" of sciences, seen as contingent and linked to socio-political events, yet irreversible.
- History as a scientific discipline, where archives act as "vetos" against arbitrary narratives.
- The "new history of sciences" (since the 1980s) is a "socio-history" that examines institutions, material culture, practices, workers, image fabrication, knowledge-power dynamics, and domination relations (class, race, gender). Examples like Galileo, Newton, Darwin, Kelvin, and Einstein are cited to show how savoirs (knowledge) and savants (scholars) are inextricably linked to their political, technical, economic, and social contexts.

The purpose of the history of sciences is multifaceted:
1. Positive history: a chronological account of facts and events, including discoveries, inventions, and the lives/institutions of scholars, placed within their social, political, and economic contexts. It aims to provide insights and inspiration for present research.
2. Evolutionary history: explores how the nature of science changes, focusing on science as a set of practices and the importance of institutions in organizing relations among scholars and constituting "proof regimes". It tracks the evolution of terms for practitioners (natural philosophers, savants, scientists). This approach addresses questions like how knowledge is produced (and non-knowledge, via funding), how proofs are established, how theory and experience evolve, how science influences the world, the role of practices (often poorly transmitted), the contributions of artisans and technicians, the links between science and technology (e.g., technoscience, colonialism, capitalism), and the relations between science and religions.

The chapter concludes by discussing icons of science, noting that images of solitary geniuses (e.g., Einstein) often date from the 19th century and contrast with the reality of collective work (e.g., Manhattan Project). Public spaces often celebrate historical figures of science, art, and politics to build national unity.

Finally, the relationship between science and technique is explored:
- Scientific knowledge is not always formalized (know-how), but knowledge that travels well (in time and space) is written.
- Derek de Solla Price's distinction: science produces texts via techniques; techniques produce objects via texts.
- Science produces verifiable statements; technique constructs means of intervention.
- "Technique" (τέχνη, technè) refers to material production or art, often associated with professional know-how. Aristotle distinguished technè from phronesis (moral knowledge) and episteme (eternal knowledge), placing technè as "morally neutral".
- The text challenges the idea of the neutrality of technique, arguing that it's not merely an application of science. Historical technical marvels existed with little documentation. Technologies evolve through a Lamarckian scheme.
- "Technology" initially meant the study of techniques but now refers to techniques as a systemic whole, implying that their neutrality can be questioned. Large technical systems (e.g., Mumford's Megamachine) shape lifestyles and ethical/political choices. Any technical choice is thus ecological, social, and political, creating a social environment from which it's hard to escape. The lack of democratic discussion on technical development is highlighted.

The chapter's ultimate conclusion is that scientificity is not defined by a single criterion but a bundle of criteria (justification, measurement, repeatability, refutability, prediction, coherence, etc.). It emphasizes the study of "knowledge regimes," which are heterogeneous assemblages of institutions, beliefs, practices, and political/economic regulations that define the place and mode of being of sciences. Examples include academies, the fundamental/applied science distinction, and innovation-centered regimes.
