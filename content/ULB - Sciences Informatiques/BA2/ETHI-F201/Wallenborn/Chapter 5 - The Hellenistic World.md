This chapter explores the Hellenistic World, characterized by the expansion of Greek culture following Alexander the Great's conquests, leading to a confrontation of Greek and "barbarian" knowledge. The intellectual center shifted from Athens to Alexandria, notably under Ptolemy I, who aimed to make it the cultural capital. Around 280 BCE, Ptolemy I established the Museum (Museîon), which housed a university, an academy, and the famous Library of Alexandria, estimated to contain between 400,000 and 700,000 volumes. The Library's goal was to gather universal knowledge, acquiring texts through purchase, seizure, and copying. The Museum attracted scholars, providing them with instruments, collections, and zoological/botanical gardens. The Library's physical traces are lost, and it served to glorify the sovereign, with Pergamum as a competing intellectual center. The Hellenistic period concluded with the Roman conquest of Egypt.

The chapter outlines arguments for the Earth's sphericity and centrality, which were accepted during this time: Earth as a natural center, circular lunar eclipses, ships disappearing over the horizon, changing stars with latitude, and Eratosthenes' calculation of Earth's circumference (45,000 km). The Farnese Atlas (2nd CE marble copy of 3rd BCE Greek original) is noted as one of the oldest known celestial globes depicting constellations.

The evolution of cosmological theory during the Hellenistic period is detailed:
- Eudoxus's concentric spheres, while a geometric model, were quickly abandoned because they failed to explain the changing distance and luminosity of planets.
- Around 200 BCE, Apollonius of Perga introduced the theory of epicycles and deferents, where a planet moves on an epicycle whose center moves on a deferent around Earth. This explained retrograde motion and luminosity variations and was mathematically simpler.
- Contact with Babylonian astronomy (from 3rd BCE) brought new questions, as Babylonians could quantitatively predict planetary phenomena from observations, something Greek theory lacked.
- Aristarchus of Samos (3rd BCE) proposed a heliocentric model (Earth orbiting the Sun), which was more economical but faced objections of sacrilege, contradiction of natural movement, and the lack of observed stellar parallax.
- Hipparchus (2nd BCE) attempted to improve the theory by off-centering the deferent but it remained insufficient.

Ptolemy's Astronomy (Claudius Ptolemy, ~90 - ~168 CE), a Greek astronomer and astrologer in Alexandria, is a key figure. His major work, Almagest (originally Books of the Mathematical Composition), built on earlier theories. Ptolemy introduced the equant point, a third center from which the epicycle's center appeared to move at uniform speed, even though its actual speed around the deferent was non-uniform. This complete theory (epicycle, eccentric deferent, equant) was highly successful, enabling precise calculation of planetary positions from a geometric theory for the first time. While the equant point broke with Aristotle's physics, it worked well and was not overly complicated, so Ptolemy's methods were used for practical planetary calculations until the Renaissance. His system "saved the phenomena". The Antikythera Mechanism (~60 BCE), an ancient astronomical calculator, is mentioned as a testament to the period's mechanical ingenuity.

Ptolemy's Geography (~150 CE) was an eight-volume manual with maps based on mathematical principles, using latitude and longitude. It contained over 8,000 place names, ranging from Iceland to Korea, and was among the most precise world maps for centuries, though his original drawn maps have not survived.

Euclid of Alexandria (~300 BCE) is highlighted for his seminal work, The Elements, an axiomatic-deductive system that systematically presented geometry and theoretical arithmetic from axioms and postulates with rigorous proofs. This work has been the basis of mathematics education for centuries. Reviel Netz's material analysis emphasizes the invention of formalism and diagrams, showing mathematics as a practice of writing, using a limited vocabulary and repetitive formulas to create an "economic system". The Greek cultural context of constant polemics drove the construction of an uncontroversial domain through abstraction. The small community of mathematicians in the Hellenistic world is noted.

Archimedes (287-212 BCE) from Syracuse, is celebrated as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time. He is known for his method of exhaustion, precise calculation of pi, study of spirals, volumes of revolution, and expression of large numbers. He notably applied mechanical concepts to geometry, particularly in statics (levers) and hydrostatics (floating bodies). The famous "Eureka" anecdote about proving a crown's gold purity is mentioned. Archimedes is also a key example of the science-war relationship, as he developed early forms of artillery (balista) and is fabled to have used "burning mirrors" during the siege of Syracuse (212 BCE) to burn Roman ships, a story contested by Descartes and even tested by MIT.

Mechanics and technology are discussed through the lens of Pappus of Alexandria (4th CE), who described theoretical (geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, physics) and practical (metalwork, carpentry) aspects. He listed five categories of mechanical arts: pulley constructors, war instrument makers, hydraulic machine makers, "prodigy" makers (e.g., Hero's pneumatics and automatons), and sphere constructors (celestial models). This highlights a valorization of war engines, useful machines, and entertaining devices.

Vitruvius (~90 - ~15 BCE), a Roman architect and engineer, is important for his work De Architectura, which provides knowledge on Roman technologies like drainage, aqueducts, materials, and central heating. His work, rare for its technical focus, was largely disregarded by literati, reflecting the low status of technical knowledge. However, it greatly influenced Renaissance engineers, helping to elevate the status of artisans and engineers.

Hero of Alexandria (1st CE) is known for his Pneumatics, describing machines and automatons (like the clepsydra, aeolipile, and automatic theater) often designed for "astonishment and wonder" or religious ceremonies. He also investigated the existence of a vacuum. His work demonstrated ingenuity, theoretical understanding of mechanisms, and applications for both utility and marvel.

The chapter notes a gradual decline of scholars at the end of antiquity:
- Few Roman contributions to sciences, except in medicine (Galen) and geography (Strabo).
- Pliny the Elder (23-79 CE) compiled the encyclopedic Natural History, a key reference for centuries. He is also noted for his proto-environmentalist criticisms of human greed exploiting Earth's depths.
- Early dissections of the human body in Alexandria were abandoned.
- Galen (129-201 CE) created a monumental encyclopedic work covering medicine (anatomy, physiology, pathology, therapeutics, pharmacology, hygiene) and philosophy, which dominated medicine until the 18th century. His work was lost in Western Europe for centuries but preserved in Byzantium and the Muslim world, returning through Arabic translations.
- Around 200 CE, scientific efforts shifted to preserving knowledge rather than new contributions, leading to uncritical transmission of texts and amplification of errors. Knowledge declined more rapidly in the Latin West than the Greek East (e.g., Boethius). Byzantium focused on theology, politics, and law, with less emphasis on natural sciences.

Imperial philosophies and religions shaped the intellectual landscape:
- Stoicism (Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, Seneca) and Epicureanism (Lucretius) were philosophies for troubled times, seeking ataraxia (peace of mind). Epicureanism adopted atomism, denied final causes or afterlife, and saw gods as uninvolved in human affairs. Stoicism viewed the cosmos as an organism with God as its soul, emphasizing absolute necessity and universal solidarity, and freedom through acting rationally. Both philosophies linked general theories of nature to ethics and theology, viewing understanding nature as a means to inner peace.
- Neoplatonism (Plotinus) also emerged.
- Christianity, especially after Constantine's Edict (313 CE) and his conversion, became a state religion and power. It showed disinterest, even hostility, towards knowledge of nature. Saint Augustine (354-430 CE) famously argued that Christians need only believe in the Creator's goodness, not details of natural science. In 529 CE, Justinian closed the School of Athens due to paganism, causing philosophers to flee to Persia.

Hypatia (~355/370 - 415 CE), a Neoplatonist philosopher, astronomer, and mathematician in Alexandria, headed the Neoplatonic school and was the first documented female mathematician. She offered public, free teaching and commented on mathematical and astronomical works, likely practicing theurgy (magic). She remained celibate and, as a non-Christian in a polytheistic environment (which was more favorable to women), taught many Christian students. Her assassination in 415 CE by a Christian mob, fueled by rumors during a conflict between the prefect Orestes (whom she advised) and Bishop Cyril, shocked the Empire and made her a "martyr of philosophy". Her symbol was later reappropriated by Christians and then by Enlightenment thinkers opposing Catholicism, becoming a romantic figure in the 19th century and a subject of film in the 20th.

Isidore of Seville (570-636 CE) is described as a major scholar whose Etymologiae included a T-O world map, a regression from Ptolemy but a common form, oriented East. He believed Earth was spherical but stars were lit by the sun.

The chapter concludes with a discussion of the fall of the Roman Empire, citing causes ranging from barbarian invasions and politico-religious factors (19th century views) to economic factors (20th century) and, more recently, environmental factors (21st century). Kyle Harper's The Fate of Rome (2017) uses evidence from glaciology, dendrochronology, palynology, climatology, and epidemiology to link climate and disease to the Empire's end. Causes of climate change include astronomical parameters, solar activity, El Niño oscillations, and volcanic eruptions. The Roman Climatic Optimum was followed by deforestation and numerous epidemics (Antonine Plague, Cyprian Plague), exacerbated by poor hygiene and vast trade networks, leading to significant population reduction and influencing the rise of Christianity. Volcanic eruptions in 536, 540, and 547 CE led to "volcanic winters" and crop failures. The Justinianic Plague (541 CE) caused a drastic population decline and, combined with climate change, fostered a sense of apocalypse, contributing to the rise of Islam. Economic transformation around 640 CE is also noted.
