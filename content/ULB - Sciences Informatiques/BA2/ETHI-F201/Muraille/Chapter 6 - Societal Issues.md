Chapter 6, "Problématiques sociétales" (Societal issues), explores the practical implications of scientific knowledge, particularly the challenges posed by pseudoscience in public health, the complex interplay between religion, politics, and science, and the critical role of ethics in scientific endeavors.

**6.1 Pseudosciences and Public Health:** The Example of Homeopathy: Pseudoscience is defined as a discipline presented with scientific appearances but lacking scientific methodology or recognition. Characteristics often include theoretical discourse without empirical validation, reliance on authority arguments, accusations of ethnocentric bias in modern science, claims of "local knowledge," denial of refuting studies, and assertions of intuition as a source of truth or multiple realities. Homeopathy, despite significant public support (57% in France believing it's as effective as conventional medicine), serves as a primary example.

Homeopathy, invented by Samuel Hahnemann in 1796, is based on the "law of similars" (diluted substances treating similar symptoms) and "law of infinitesimals" (greater dilution equals greater efficacy), leading to extreme dilutions where no original substance remains. Its rise coincided with the harmful "heroic medicine" of the 19th century, making it seem less damaging by comparison. Homeopathy gained popularity in the West, then in India, where it became institutionalized as a national medicine, sometimes linked to anti-colonial sentiment. It was even granted official recognition and reimbursement in France despite no scientific mechanism of action. Jacques Benveniste's "water memory" experiments (1988), though later refuted as irreproducible and biased by industry funding, were used to legitimize homeopathy.

The rise of Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) in the 1990s-2000s rigorously evaluated homeopathy through large-scale clinical trials and meta-analyses, consistently demonstrating no efficacy beyond the placebo effect. The placebo effect, a real psycho-physiological response dependent on patient beliefs and expectations, can be significant, especially for pain, anxiety, and depression. Its mechanisms, linked to conditioning and positive patient expectations, involve brain activity changes and hormone production affecting pain and immune responses. EBM aims to identify treatments whose efficacy is significantly superior to the placebo effect.

Despite overwhelming evidence against its specific efficacy, political complacency and financial interests (a multi-million Euro industry) often protect homeopathy. The debate highlights issues of individual choice in a mutualized healthcare system (where everyone pays for ineffective treatments), the risk of delaying conventional treatments (increasing mortality for serious conditions like cancer), and the dangerous interplay between private financial interests, political agendas, and public susceptibility to pseudoscience.

**6.2 Religion, Politics, and Science – Academic Freedom:** Governments often legitimize their actions through "explanatory doctrines" (religious or political ideologies) that influence knowledge production. Throughout history, science has challenged religious views (e.g., geocentrism vs. heliocentrism, creationism vs. evolution), leading to conflicts. The trials against astronomers like Giordano Bruno, Johannes Kepler, and Galileo Galilei illustrate the Church's attempts to suppress scientific theories contradicting biblical texts. Similarly, in the USA, religious fundamentalists have continuously fought against the teaching of evolution, attempting to ban it, mandate "creation science," or promote "intelligent design" (a pseudoscientific argument for God's existence). These efforts have been challenged in court, with rulings confirming creationism as religion, not science. The "academic freedom" strategy allows "objective criticism" of scientific theories in schools.

The concept of secularization of science (independence of science and religion) emerged from these conflicts, particularly in Europe. The Catholic Church has shifted from fidéisme (truth only from God's word) to concordism (interpreting the Bible to align with science) and, more recently, to acknowledging evolution as more than a hypothesis and rehabilitating figures like Galileo. Stephen Jay Gould proposed NOMA (Nonoverlapping Magisteria), advocating for mutual respect between science and religion, each having distinct domains of authority (facts for science, morals for religion). However, some scientists (e.g., Richard Dawkins) reject NOMA, advocating for "New Atheism" or "scientism," where science is the sole source of knowledge. Conversely, some traditionalists use science to "prove" religious beliefs (inverted concordism). Fideism, particularly in some evangelical Protestant and Islamic communities, explicitly rejects the Enlightenment's emphasis on reason and secularism, prioritizing biblical infallibility or religious texts as ultimate sources of truth, leading to a decline in evolution education in some countries. Globally, a significant portion of the population trusts religion over science when they contradict, especially outside Europe, and there's a correlation between high religiosity and lower economic development, though with exceptions.

The section also examines the Stalinist conflict against genetics (Lysenkoism), where agricultural policies in the USSR under Trofim Lysenko banned modern genetics as "bourgeois science" and promoted the inheritance of acquired characteristics as "proletarian biology". This ideological intervention led to the persecution of geneticists and devastating famines in Russia and China, illustrating the dangers of mixing political ideology and science.

These historical struggles underscore the importance of academic freedom, which protects researchers' liberty to investigate, teach, and communicate without religious or political interference. This principle, championed by figures like Francis Bacon, the Humboldtian model, and the AAUP, is seen as crucial for the free pursuit of truth and societal well-being. The Magna Charta Universitatum (1988) further codifies the moral and intellectual independence of universities. The ULB's commitment to "libre examen" embodies this spirit, promoting independent judgment, rational analysis, and confrontation with facts over authority or dogma.

**6.3 Ethics and Science:** The necessity of ethical control over knowledge production and technological innovations gained prominence in the second half of the 20th century. This involves ethical review of research subjects, practices (e.g., animal experimentation, clinical studies), and the societal impacts of innovations, especially those with dual-use potential (civilian and military applications). However, anticipating all research outcomes and societal impacts is difficult, and varying national legislations and corporate competition pose obstacles to ethical regulation.

Ethics is a philosophical discipline on moral judgments, distinct from morality (categorical imperatives of right/wrong). Ethics, unlike morality, is a critical reflection on values. Its foundations are not necessarily based on verifiable scientific reality or even rationality. Traditionally, three major moral theories exist:
1. **Virtue Ethics (Aristotle):** Focuses on optimal self-realization, promoting virtues like courage, generosity, moderation, and justice. Critiqued for subjectivity and limited guidance on decisions impacting others.
2. **Deontological Ethics (Kant):** Emphasizes satisfying duties (to God, self, others) based on universal reason, regardless of consequences. Actions are judged by their motivations, not outcomes. Critiqued for ignoring consequences.
3. **Consequentialist Ethics (Bentham's Utilitarianism):** Judges acts solely on their consequences, aiming to maximize pleasure and minimize pain ("greatest good for the greatest number"). Critiqued for difficulty in measuring pleasure/pain, complexity of consequences, and potential disregard for minority well-being.

Moral psychology studies factors influencing these choices, with deontological judgments often linked to emotions and utilitarian ones to reasoning or lack of empathy. Famous moral dilemmas like the "trolley problem" illustrate the tension between these ethical frameworks.

The chapter also briefly introduces other ethical perspectives:
- **Spinoza's "Ethics Demonstrated in Geometrical Order":** Argues for absolute materialism and determinism, where reason leads to freedom and virtue by understanding causal laws and not being dominated by emotions.
- **Nietzsche's "Slave Morality":** Critiques Judeo-Christian morality as a system created by the weak to limit the powerful, stemming from a rejection of life and a desire to reverse natural values. He advocates for a "Superman" who embodies the "will to power" and transcends conventional morality.
- **Singer's Antispécisme:** A utilitarian philosopher who argues that if animals share human sensitivity, they deserve equal consideration, equating "speciesism" to racism or sexism. This perspective can lead to controversial stances on issues like infanticide or euthanasia, based on the value of life determined by sensitivity and consciousness.
- **Discussion Ethics (Apel and Habermas):** A recent ethical theory that, acknowledging the plurality of values in modern societies, proposes a method for rational debate to achieve consensus on ethical norms. It suggests that only norms agreed upon by all participants in a practical discussion can claim validity. This framework relies on an optimistic view of rational, unmanipulated debate.

The chapter concludes by emphasizing the vast diversity of ethical systems, the challenge of applying ethics in multicultural societies, and the fact that there isn't "one ethics," but many.
