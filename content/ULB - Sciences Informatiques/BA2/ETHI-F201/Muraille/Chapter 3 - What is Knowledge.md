Chapter 3, "Qu’est-ce que la connaissance ?" (What is knowledge?), focuses on the core epistemological problem of differentiating belief from knowledge and determining the value (local or universal) of knowledge.

It explores several distinct, often opposing, epistemological positions: idealism, realism, relativism, and constructivism, noting that these largely derive from adopted ontological positions. Ontology, the study of what is and the nature of reality, influences the method of knowledge acquisition.

The chapter stresses that a foundation of ontological beliefs, often tied to religious, philosophical, or political ideologies, inevitably underpins how scientific knowledge is produced.

**Idealism:** This ontological position, adhered to by only a small percentage of current philosophers, generally considers the mind as the ultimate foundation of all reality, where the perception of an object is necessary for its existence.

Epistemologically, idealism posits that knowledge is produced by rational reasoning and is inherently determined by the structure of human thought.
- Platonic idealism, for instance, distinguishes between a material world of appearances and an unchanging world of ideas, asserting that only reason can access the true essence of things.
- Descartes' "problematic idealism" questions the existence of the external world, emphasizing intuition and deduction as sources of certainty.
- Berkeley's subjective idealism, or immaterialism, radically claims that only ideas exist, and matter exists only through ideas (esse est percipi aut percipere).
	- These forms of idealism can lead to solipsism, where only the thinking subject's existence is certain.
- Kant's transcendental idealism suggests we only perceive "phenomena" (representations of reality), not "noumena" (reality in itself), implying that our knowledge is shaped by our mind's categories.
- Hegel's absolute idealism, in contrast, posits an identity between thought and being, viewing reason as an objective principle.
- Later idealist movements, like Schopenhauer's metaphysical pessimism and Nietzsche's will to power, explore the irrational and fundamental drives behind reality.

Ultimately, idealism is characterized by a dualistic (matter/spirit) or monistic (spirit-only) ontology, skepticism towards the material world, and an anthropocentric view where human thought often creates or shapes reality.

**Realism:** This position, favored by a large majority of philosophers, proposes that the physical world has a determined material structure independent of human conceptualization, rejecting mind-matter dualism in favor of material monism.

Epistemological realism is optimistic about humanity's capacity to produce true theories that describe reality, believing in a "scientific method" that can objectively apprehend facts, free from socio-cultural biases. It maintains that scientific knowledge is universal, independent of "non-epistemic values" (e.g., morality, religion, politics). This aligns with the "value-free science ideal," although values can influence research direction and ethical constraints.

The roots of realism lie in the 18th-century Enlightenment, 19th-century positivism and naturalism, and 20th-century neo-positivism and Karl Popper's philosophy. Realism views science as a driver of societal progress through improved understanding and innovation.

**Relativism:** While not denying an independently structured physical world, relativism asserts that science's progress isn't necessarily towards a better understanding of the world, being instead dependent on social context.
- Thomas Kuhn's moderate relativism, based on the sociology of scientific knowledge, suggests that scientific paradigms shift due to social factors, not always leading to progress towards truth.
- Paul Feyerabend's radical relativism, or "epistemological anarchism," posits that the world's complexity makes any single scientific methodology insufficient, advocating for a plurality of methods ("anything goes"). This radical view holds that all theories or viewpoints are equally valid, even pseudosciences, and challenges the notion of universal rationality.

Critics argue that extreme relativism leads to internal contradictions and renders scientific endeavor meaningless.

**Constructivism:** Constructivism emerged as a critique of realism, attempting to avoid radical relativism while maintaining a moderate relativist stance. Its ontology acknowledges an observer-independent reality but claims that human knowledge of it is biased/subjective.

Epistemologically, constructivism argues that knowledge is constructed by the subject, emphasizing the role of social structures in shaping our understanding of reality, leading to "local knowledge" valid within specific communities.

This movement is deeply intertwined with political activism, tracing its roots to Kantian and Hegelian idealism, Marxism, the Frankfurt School, postmodernism, feminist studies, and postcolonial studies. It challenges the idea of universal, objective science and highlights how knowledge can be a tool of social domination.

**Conflict between Modern and Postmodern Philosophy: Science Wars:** This section highlights the clash between modern/realist philosophy and postmodern/constructivist/relativist philosophy.
- Modern philosophy, tied to the Enlightenment, views the world as stable and knowable through rational observation and experimentation, leading to social progress.
- Postmodern philosophy, conversely, rejects this, seeing the world as fragmented and knowledge as socially constructed, often serving as a tool for social domination.

This conflict, peaking in the 1990s with events like the Sokal affair, has led to accusations of intellectual imposture against postmodernists. While natural sciences tend towards realism, human sciences lean towards constructivism, reflecting differences in their objects of study and methods.
