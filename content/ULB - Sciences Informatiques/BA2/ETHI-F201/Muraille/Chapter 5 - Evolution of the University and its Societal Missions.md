Chapter 5, "Evolution de l’université et de ses missions sociétales" (Evolution of the university and its societal missions), traces the historical development of universities and their changing roles, heavily influenced by funding sources and societal expectations.

**5.1 The Medieval University and Academies:** Universities emerged in Europe during the High Middle Ages (10th-11th centuries) from cathedral and monastery schools, initially under the Church's monopoly to ensure religious orthodoxy. They aimed to train literate individuals for public and private affairs, with Latin as the official language and an emphasis on oral teaching from authoritative texts (sacred texts, Roman law, Greek authors). Major universities like Bologna, Paris, and Oxford formed in the 13th century as self-help communities for masters and scholars, often under papal protection, granting them autonomy from local bishops but still subject to Church authority (e.g., the 1270 condemnation of Aristotelian propositions at Paris). From the 16th to 18th centuries, universities became more nationalized and fragmented due to religious schisms and increased state funding, leading to a decline in student mobility and a shift towards training state servants. The humanist movement criticized the medieval university model for its limited practical teaching and lack of scientific research. In response, states created independent institutions like academies (e.g., Collège Royal in 1530) for scientific exchange and open science, and professional schools for practical knowledge (e.g., surgery, engineering). Scientific communication during this period relied on letters and the first scientific journals (e.g., Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society from 1665). In essence, medieval universities primarily focused on teaching and religious conformity, with little research, which largely occurred outside their walls in academies.

**5.2 The Modern University:** The Humboldtian Model: The "golden age of academia" emerged in 19th-century Prussia with the Humboldtian model, championed by Wilhelm von Humboldt. Following Prussia's defeat by France, there was a consensus that economic and technological advancement required educational reform. This model aimed to integrate teaching, research, and research training within the university, making it a professional research institution. Research results were publicly shared (open science), and knowledge was considered a "common good," produced disinterestedly and without utilitarian concerns. The university was state-funded but largely independent, with professors appointed for life and research funded unconditionally, fostering academic freedom and long-term research. The Humboldtian model conceived of the researcher as a master driven by passion, not external incentives. While influential globally, critiques included its perceived elitism, detachment from the economy, and limited practical education. This model transformed the university from a mere corporation of teachers and students to a primary site of knowledge production, with the state guaranteeing its independence and defining a "Mode 1" of knowledge transfer to society.

**5.3 The Liberal Reform:** The Entrepreneurial University: After World War II, a new "Big Science" model emerged, exemplified by the Manhattan Project, involving unprecedented government-funded international scientific-military cooperation for fundamental and applied research. Universities faced crises post-1945: massification of higher education in the 1960s-79s, followed by economic crises (oil shocks) and liberal austerity policies from the 1980s onwards. In response, reforms, particularly in the UK under Margaret Thatcher, drastically cut public funding, allowed universities to charge higher fees, ended lifetime employment for professors, and introduced performance-based funding and competition among universities. Principles of "New Public Management" imposed accountability, external objective-setting, and a focus on producing industry-relevant graduates and innovations. European reforms, driven by organizations like the European Round Table of Industrialists (ERT) and formalized in the Lisbon Strategy and Bologna Process, further pushed universities towards economic development through scientific innovation, prioritizing "useful knowledge". Commercial publishers gained control over scientific journals, and while the internet facilitated open access, a concentration of power among a few large publishers emerged. This liberal reform effectively dismantled the Humboldtian model, making universities competitive, market-driven entities whose value is determined by their utility to society and business, marking a new mode of knowledge production.

**5.4 Modes of Knowledge Production:** In the "knowledge society," producing "useful knowledge" aligned with corporate needs became a university priority. Gibbons (1994) introduced Mode 1 (knowledge produced in universities and transferred to industry) and Mode 2 (innovations from cooperative interactions between universities, industries, and government). Mode 2, seen as producing broader, economically and socially useful knowledge, is described as "best science". Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff (2000) further developed this with the "Triple Helix model" (Mode 3), arguing that Mode 1 was a transient, unnatural system to protect science from political corruption, and Mode 2 represents the natural format for knowledge production. The Triple Helix model envisions blurring boundaries between government, universities, and industry, justifying the transformation of universities into commercial entities focused on innovation and industry-aligned graduates. This can lead to difficulties in defining conflicts of interest and promotes a "democracy of knowledge" where all societal components are integrated. Carayannis (2012-14) extended this to the "Quintuple Helix," integrating media, public culture, and the natural environment. The dominant theories now suggest optimal innovation requires universities to be integrated into enterprise networks, adopting business norms and losing their independent status.

**5.5 Evolution of Funding Strategies:** Research funding strategies have shifted from "sprinkling" (Mode 1 logic, funding all research areas) to an "excellence strategy" (Mode 2 logic, funding only useful or visible projects). This shift, driven by reduced funding and liberal reforms, means only projects deemed "excellent" are supported, leading to intense competition among researchers. Metrics like the impact factor (journal prestige based on citations) and the h-index (researcher productivity and visibility) are used to quantify research quality. However, these metrics are problematic, as high citations don't guarantee scientific quality, and famous papers might not be highly cited. The excellence strategy has exacerbated competition, creating strong incentives to publish quickly in high-impact journals, increasing the potential for fraud.

**5.6 Consequences of the Excellence Strategy and Scientific Fraud:** Scientific research has always been competitive, but the excellence strategy has intensified it, making careers dependent on single publications in prestigious journals. This has led to a proliferation of "hyper-prolific" scientists and a concentration of funding in a few "rich clubs" of leading universities, creating an "effect Matthieu" (those who have, receive more) and reduced interdisciplinarity. Alarmingly, this has correlated with a decline in disruptive innovations and an increase in scientific fraud and retractions, which have exploded in recent years. Most retractions are due to fraud (e.g., data fabrication, image manipulation, plagiarism, algorithm-generated articles), not accidental errors. Fraud can have deadly consequences, as seen in the case of Joachim Boldt, whose fabricated data led to harmful medical practices. The rise of predatory journals, which accept articles for publication and authors' fees without promised quality control, further blurs the line between legitimate science and intellectual imposture.

**5.7 Researcher Deontology:** Researcher deontology refers to the duties of researchers, defined by ethical rules from the scientific community itself (ethos of science) and societal ethical norms. Scientific integrity means conforming to these norms and preventing fraud. Key historical texts include the Nuremberg Code (1947) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), which established principles for human experimentation (e.g., informed consent, avoiding harm, ethical committee review). More recent guidelines like the Singapore Statement on Research Integrity (2010) and the European Charter for Researchers (2005) emphasize honesty, objectivity, data sharing, conflict of interest declarations, and reporting misconduct. Modern deontological norms require researchers to declare conflicts of interest and be responsible for their publications, effectively excluding anonymous publishing. While these norms are often not legally binding, they aim to ensure reliability, rigor, and accountability in scientific activities.
