This chapter begins by exploring knowledge before science, starting with Homo sapiens (150-200 thousand years ago) and their cohabitation with Neanderthals. The domestication of fire, appearing around 1 million years ago, is highlighted as a uniquely human technique that transformed humans by allowing new forms of alimentation (more energy for body and brain), protection, and hardening of tools. Fire eventually led to metallurgy and motor power. Early techniques often left few apparent traces, but scientists can now reconstruct ancient flora, diets, and climates. The document notes that historical images of these early periods are often gendered, inherited from the 19th century.

Recent advancements and debates in anthropology and archaeology, particularly critiques of linear history narratives (e.g., Harari, Scott, Graeber & Wengrow), are mentioned. These critiques challenge the classical view of civilization as a natural, inevitable progression from agriculture, to industry, to democracy. The idea of evolution and progress, it is noted, is largely a 19th-century construct.

The Neolithic Revolution marks a period of progressive mastery over "nature". Similar ecological conditions during the Holocene (starting ~12,000 years ago) led to the independent rise of urbanism in different parts of the world around the same time. This era saw profound technical and social mutations, including agriculture and animal husbandry, leading to sedentarization. Key technical innovations include polished stone tools, pottery, spinning, weaving, and architectural development. The first great urbanization centers emerged near major water sources like Mesopotamia (Tigris and Euphrates), Egypt (Nile), Indus, and Yellow River. Lists of domesticated plants (e.g., wheat, rice, potatoes, corn) and animals (e.g., dog, goat, sheep, cattle, pig, cat, chicken, horse) illustrate the scale of this transformation. The text provocatively asks: "Domestication or creation of species?".

The chapter then discusses the first usages of metals, providing approximate dates for copper, gold, silver, iron (meteoritic), lead, tin, bronze, mercury, and the smelting of iron ore. These first seven metals were known to Mesopotamians, Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans, associated with civilizations, planets, and alchemy. The later discovery of other metals is also listed.

The first States are presented as documented through the "history of victors". Their emergence involved hydraulic irrigation systems requiring large-scale cooperation, leading to the creation of cities and specialization of labor. Writing (Sumerian cuneiform, ~3200 BCE), primarily for accounting, and geometry (for land measurement) were developed. Urban life was enabled by increased agricultural productivity providing food surplus. Early medicine (e.g., Egyptian surgery, trepanation) and astronomical observations for structuring rhythms (especially agricultural) are also noted. James C. Scott's Homo Domesticus is cited for its critique of "progress," arguing that the Neolithic era led to a deterioration of living conditions (health, epidemics, slavery, taxes).

The beginnings of astronomy are traced back to the observation of celestial bodies. Early civilizations like the Babylonians conducted numerous astronomical observations, believing in relationships between the microcosm and macrocosm, allowing for predictions. However, Babylonian theories were not geometric and lacked a philosophical inquiry into the causes of phenomena, unlike Greek astronomy that emerged later. The phenomenon of retrograde motion of planets and their cycles were observed and cataloged.

The earliest known maps and geography are discussed, with the oldest known world map being a Babylonian clay tablet from ~600 BCE, depicting a flat disc surrounded by an ocean, blending real geography with Babylonian cosmology.

Myths are defined as stories about origins (world, humans, animals, customs) that justify existence and provide meaning. They show rather than demonstrate, and are not based on reasoning.

The "Greek Miracle" is introduced as a 19th-century term for the intellectual and artistic effervescence of 5th-century BCE Athens (Parthenon, philosophy, history, theater, sculpture), alongside new political questions like currency, slavery, and democracy. Independent Greek city-states competed but exchanged goods and ideas. Raphael's "School of Athens" fresco (1509-1511) is presented as a Renaissance reinterpretation of ancient Greek philosophy and "knowledge of causes".

The Presocratics (6th-5th centuries BCE) are then explored, known mainly through fragments cited by their adversaries (Plato, Aristotle) and later historians. This highlights that their history is filtered. Two main schools are identified:
- Ionians (Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus) from cities like Miletus, focused on principles of nature.
- Italics (Pythagoreans, Eleatics like Parmenides, Zeno of Elea) from Southern Italy and Sicily, focused on metaphysical speculation.
- Thales of Miletus (~625 - ~547 BCE) is considered the first philosopher of nature, a geometer and astronomer. He is believed to have borrowed much from Egyptians and Mesopotamians but was the first to write a treatise on phusis (nature). Thales opposed phusiologoi (those explaining nature rationally) to theologoi (those explaining via gods), establishing a fundamental distinction between natural and supernatural. He offered rational explanations for celestial phenomena, like the Moon eclipsing the Sun, rather than divine anger. His core idea was that the primordial element is water, and Earth floats on it, explaining earthquakes as water agitation. Aristotle's summary of Thales' reasoning for water as the principle (arkhê) is provided, noting water's three forms. The myth of Thales falling in a well while observing the stars is mentioned. Presocratics generally asked "What is being?", "What is movement?", "What is knowledge?". Their universal affirmations led to discussion and competition between theories.
- Anaximander of Miletus (~610 - ~546 BCE) was possibly the first to use "arkhê" for philosophical inquiry, defining it as a perpetual, continually generating origin. His principle was the apeiron (infinite/unlimited), the universal, indestructible, permanent cause. He believed the universe originated from the separation of opposites (hot/cold, dry/humid) from primordial matter through a "whirlwind". He also proposed spontaneous generation of living beings from a moist, earthy substance, with humans evolving from aquatic animals, a "first theory of evolution". His astronomical model posited celestial bodies as fire rings and the Earth "suspended without support" equidistant from all things.

Pythagoras (~590 - ~497 BCE) and his followers were known for mystical beliefs (immortality of souls) and their political influence. While his theorem was known earlier, the Pythagoreans emphasized mathematics as a spiritual exercise. Their core belief was "Everything is number," meaning number constitutes the intelligible structure of things and that mathematical elements are elements of things. They focused on arithmetic, geometry, music theory, and astronomy, famously finding numerical ratios in musical intervals. They were more interested in the mathematical unification of the cosmos (theory and perfection) than detailed observations, leading to the "harmony of the spheres". This Greek alliance of astronomy, arithmetic, music, and magic was influential until the 17th century.

The problem of change became a central issue in 5th-century BCE natural sciences, questioning how to describe change with fixed elements. This led to debates about the reliability of senses versus reason and the fundamental nature of knowledge (epistemology) and change (ontology). The conflict between permanence (Parmenides) and change (Heraclitus) emerged as a core opposition, with the former influencing the scientific approach that "cuts" and "fixes" the flux of things.

The Eleatics, particularly Parmenides (late 6th - mid 5th BCE), asserted "Being is, non-being is not," positing that change is an illusion and becoming does not exist. He established ontology (science of Being), arguing that reason (logos) dictates Being is intelligible, uncreated, timeless, continuous, and spherical. His disciple, Zeno of Elea (~490 - ~430 BCE), presented paradoxes of motion (dichotomy, Achilles and the tortoise, the flying arrow) not to deny movement, but to show that those who defend its divisibility are inconsistent, thereby supporting Parmenides' view of a unified, continuous world.

Heraclitus (~544 - ~480 BCE) is famous for "Panta rhei" ("all things flow") and "You never step twice in the same river," emphasizing universal mobility, the necessary struggle of opposites, and their identity. He believed fire was the arkhê, the principle and god, representing the reality of movement and the cosmos's first and last state. His saying "Nature loves to hide" implies deciphering its secrets and the ephemeral nature of appearances.

Empedocles (~490 - ~435 BCE) proposed two cyclical principles: Friendship/Love (unifying) and Hate/Strife (dividing), which act on four elements: water, earth, fire, and aether (or air). He explained that a finite number of elements could form an unlimited number of substances by combining in different proportions (e.g., bone as fire, water, earth). His ideas of elemental composition influenced medicine and alchemy.

The Atomists, including Democritus (~460 - ~370 BCE) and his teacher Leucippus, proposed that nature consists of two principles: atoms ("indivisible") and the void (non-being). They argued that only atoms and void are real, resolving Parmenides' paradox by seeing becoming as a composition of being and non-being. Atomists distinguished between perceptions conforming to reality (heavy, hard, dense) and those that are unreliable (color, sound, taste, smell, temperature), a precursor to "primary and secondary qualities". They believed in an infinite quantity of invisible, solid, indivisible atoms of varying shapes, moving randomly in the infinite void, colliding, rebounding, or combining to form all compounds. Generation is a union of atoms, destruction a separation. Anaxagoras's idea of a plurality of worlds is also mentioned.

Hippocrates of Cos (~460 - ~377 BCE), traditionally seen as the "father of medicine," represented a school emphasizing clinical observation and documentation through clear, objective writing. His treatise On the Sacred Disease is emblematic for opposing rational medicine to religious/magical explanations for conditions like epilepsy. He proposed that disease is a bodily process influenced by environmental factors (air, water, places), diet, and lifestyle, presenting a new vision of humans in relation to their environment, detailed in Airs, Waters, Places. The theory of the four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile), linked to the four elements, seasons, and temperaments, became his school's most famous theory, dominating medical thought until the modern era, popularized by Galen.

The "lessons of the Presocratics" conclude the discussion: they sought an order in phusis (nature) distinct from the divine. Phusis, translated as natura, in Greek philosophy signified a process, transformations, and that which is generated and changes without divine intervention. Its absence of transcendence for truth allowed for dialogue between rival city-states. Their approach was "materialistic," based on observations independent of human or divine intentions. However, their work was largely theoria (contemplation) over praxis. The fundamental opposition between Heraclitus (change) and Parmenides (stability) is noted as a recurring theme in intellectual history. Astronomical knowledge attributed to them (zodiac obliquity, eclipses, star movements) is also mentioned.

The chapter also touches on the income sources of savants in antiquity: personal fortune for aristocrats, high earnings for renowned physicians, payment for teaching (Sophists, Academies), patronage (Hellenistic period), and mendicancy for some philosophers (Cynics). The prevailing idea was that "research on nature is its own reward," highlighting the challenge of earning a living from "contemplative life" or "useless ideas".

Plato (~428 - ~348 BCE), following Socrates, founded the Academy (~387 BCE) with the inscription "Let no one ignorant of geometry enter here". Influenced by Pythagoreans, he emphasized arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and acoustics not for utility but to guide the soul towards invisible realities. Plato's compromise between Heraclitus and Parmenides posited that ultimate reality lies in a realm of immaterial Forms or Ideas, which are more real and perfect than the imperfect world of things we perceive through senses. Mathematical objects, like a perfect circle or π, were seen as existing independently of humans. He introduced the "Demiurge," a good, non-jealous creator god, suggesting that nature imitates divine art (technology), leading to a teleological (purpose-driven) understanding of nature. Plato also developed a geometric theory of primordial elements, the Platonic Solids, associating fire with a sharp tetrahedron, air with an octahedron, water with an icosahedron, and earth with a stable cube, with the dodecahedron representing the cosmos (later "ether" by Aristotle).

Eudoxus of Cnidus (~408 - ~355 BCE), a student of Plato, developed a mathematical model of the heavens based on Plato's principles of uniform circular and geocentric movements. His model of concentric spheres for each celestial body attempted to explain planetary movements, including retrograde motion, but failed to account for changing distances to Earth or variations in planetary brightness. Heraclides of Pontus (4th century BCE) later suggested Earth's axial rotation and that Venus and Mercury might orbit the Sun.
