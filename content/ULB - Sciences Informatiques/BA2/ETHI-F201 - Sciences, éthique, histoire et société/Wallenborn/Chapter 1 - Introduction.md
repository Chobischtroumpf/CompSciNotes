The intentions of the course are to associate four key ideas: science, ethics, society, and history.
- Science is defined as knowledge produced by an open community that organizes contradictory debate, formalized for transmission and reproduction ("facts"). It's plural, encompassing exact, inexact, human, hard, soft, and natural sciences.
- Ethics involves reflection on behaviors and norms, asking "What should I do?" and examining values, acts, and consequences. Ethical themes are dispersed throughout the course, with problems becoming prominent in the 20th century.
- History explains that sciences have constantly changed and transformed. The history of science is vast, without clear beginning or end. It serves as the raw material for Wallenborn's part of the course.
- Society acknowledges that sciences are intertwined with knowledge and power, often linked to sovereigns (now businesses), embedded in contemporary societies, and have strong ties to economy and development from the modern era. It also considers power dynamics and exclusion (e.g., women, "races").

The course axes include introducing important events and figures in science history, emphasizing scientific institutions and instruments (laboratories), and constructing the modern concept of nature. It also aims to make connections across course parts and encourages thinking from the perspective of knowledge and society of a given era. The distinction between STEM/STIM (science, technology, engineering, mathematics) and SHS (human and social sciences) is introduced, with SHS being critical and reflexive, unlike STEM, which focuses on building and agreeing on facts.

A trigger warning is issued for the Anthropocene section, as it contains information that might induce or accentuate eco-anxiety, defining it as intense emotions facing ecosystem degradation, and recognizing it as a healthy reaction. The Anthropocene is presented as a new geological age driven by humanity, succeeding the Holocene, with large-scale detectable planetary modifications like climate evolution, biodiversity loss, acidification, and pollution. The "Anthropogenic Mass" is noted to have surpassed organic biomass. Its beginning is debated, with the Industrial Revolution (Crutzen) or post-WWII as proposed start dates. It's described as a process, not a single origin.

The concept of "The Great Acceleration" from 1950 is introduced in relation to socio-economic and Earth system evolutions. Planetary boundaries are discussed, highlighting that exceeding them could lead to abrupt, non-linear, unpredictable changes. Critiques of this approach include underestimation of marine areas, lack of consideration for inequalities, and concerns about an "expertocracy". Key intergovernmental bodies like the GIEC (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, created 1988) and IPBES (Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services, created 2012) are presented as synthesizers of scientific knowledge on climate change and biodiversity. The necessity of negative emissions from 2050 to limit warming to 1.5°C is highlighted.

The course addresses the question of trusting the GIEC, discussing arguments from climate skeptics (uncertainty, natural climate change, scientist bias). It emphasizes that scientific uncertainty is reduced by the organization of scientific communities and their institutions (peer review, conferences, verifiable information). Ethical questions arising from the GIEC's work, such as implicit values, geoengineering, technological solutions, equity, and democracy, are raised.

Definitions of ethics (philosophical discipline on moral judgments, values, justifications, asking "what should I do?") and morality (related to customs, character, rules of conduct) are provided. It differentiates between theoretical and applied ethics, and links ethics to politics and law. Max Weber's concepts of "ethics of responsibility" (rational towards a goal, pragmatic) and "ethics of conviction" (adhering to values/norms, scientists' ethics) are introduced. The distinction between ethics, deontology (duties of a profession), and scientific integrity (good research practice) is also made, mentioning charters, codes, and university committees for integrity.

Examples of ethical conflicts in science include clashes with religious beliefs (heliocentrism, atomism, age of Earth, evolution), considerations for humans, life, and nature (Von Humboldt and Darwin against slavery, colonial exploitation of resources), and 20th-century transformations (chemical weapons, nuclear energy, biology/medicine advancements, ecology, privacy). The neutrality and objectivity of science are questioned. A case study of the CERN's Future Circular Collider (FCC) project is used to illustrate ethical dilemmas concerning energy-intensive, costly, and environmentally damaging research with uncertain scientific returns. This raises questions about "pure" research justifying any means without citizen participation.

The course then explores three types of values attributed to biodiversity: intrinsic (importance in itself), patrimonial (cultural, aesthetic, historical interest), and instrumental/utilitarian (resources, services for economy/society). The IPBES (2019) estimate of 1 million species threatened with extinction is cited, emphasizing the degradation of ecosystems that underpin human well-being. The "critical zone" where life develops is mentioned as an example of how a species can modify the Earth system.

Different attitudes towards environmental challenges are presented:
- Collapsology (Effondriste): Inevitable collapse, systemic risks, state unable to provide basic services, population decrease. Critiques: slow process, Western-centric.
- Technosolutionism: Humanity saved by technology, "no problem without solution," era of abundance, radical form in Transhumanism (augmenting human physical/mental capacities). Critiques: solutions don't work or are too slow, costs/consequences, delays action, detached from local communities, despite some engineers opposing it.
- Degrowth (Décroissantiste): Organized reduction of activities (GDP, consumption, production, exchanges, material flows). Affirms planetary limits, resists acceleration, advocates low-tech, attention to ecosystems. Critiques: unrealistic, Malthusian, Western-centric.
- Transhumanism: Humans are imperfect, technology can improve them (eliminate aging/death, mind uploading). Convergence of nanotech, biotech, IT, cognitive sciences. Ambition for AI to have all human aptitudes. Critiques: for whom, material/energy costs, multinational interests.

The document also contrasts Anthropocene with Capitalocene (Jason Moore), noting that entropy increases as biodiversity decreases. It highlights that science and technology observe and measure Anthropocene consequences, leading to deeper understanding of problems that are worsening. History offers perspective, showing difficult periods but also knowledge transmission. The problem with the concept of nature is central to the course.

The section on energy discusses that there has "never been an energy transition" and shows a strong correlation between global GDP and energy consumption, with decoupling not observed globally. It provides a very short history of energy, from fire domestication (500,000 years ago) to ancient renewables (wood, wind, sun, hydraulic, animal traction) and the early uses of oil and coal. The modern era is characterized by a change in the scale of energy use. Ambivalence of science and technology for the environment is noted, as "modern" technologies have caused degradation, but "green" technologies, while polluting less, often drive growth, require more minerals, and produce non-recyclable electronics, increasing entropy.

The relationship between humans and their machines is discussed, including the concept of "exosomatic organs" and the idea that anthropogenic mass exceeded biomass in 2020. The challenges of metallic resources and "zombie technologies" (where dispersal of non-renewable minerals prevents recycling) are introduced.

The nature and humans section highlights a paradox: nature is independent, yet humans are progressively decentered (Copernicus, Darwin, Freud), seeming contingent. However, nature is also actively constructed and transformed by human activities. The modern concept of nature, stemming from the Greeks, distinguishes primary (objective) and secondary (subjective) qualities, enabling mathematization and the "purification" strategies of the laboratory, leading to the "devitalization of matter". Alternative views recognize living beings, their relations, and evolution as "another nature".

The course emphasizes that new sensitivities to the living world are emerging, fueled by research and ethics of the environment, but these are often normative rather than purely scientific. The idea of "Mastery or Cooperation?" is posed. Descola's "Beyond Nature and Culture" framework, using anthropology to think about human-non-human relations through modes of identification and relation (interiority, physicality), is presented, distinguishing Totemism, Naturalism, Animism, and Analogism. The performativity of matter, and how sciences change with them, is a key idea. The Anthropocene implies that separating nature from humans is no longer possible, with reciprocal effects. The course asks where to invest in science given its consequences on nature, and questions whether the structure of nature dictates knowledge (realism) or if human organization of science affects how we divide the natural world (idealism).

Finally, the document addresses how to fight eco-anxiety, suggesting that information alone isn't enough, but rather building a new world, acting individually and collectively, engaging politically, and connecting with others.
