This chapter details the "Golden Age of Islam," beginning by setting the scene in the Near East before Islam's advent, with the Persian (Sassanid) and Byzantine Empires. While European cities declined in the Early Middle Ages, maintaining literary and religious culture in monasteries, cities endured in the Eastern Roman Empire. Ctesiphon, the Sassanid capital, hosted scholars displaced from Constantinople and absorbed Indian influences like sine tables and positional numbering ("Arabic numerals").

The rise of Islam is traced from Muhammad's revelation (~600 CE) and the Hegira (622 CE) to Medina. The Muslim expansion was swift, conquering vast territories (Damascus, Jerusalem, Alexandria, Spain, etc.) within years, often welcomed by populations suffering under previous rulers. Successive caliphates, the Umayyads (650-750 CE) and then the Abbasids (who founded Baghdad, with its location chosen by astrology), governed this empire. The Umayyads maintained power in Spain (Al-Andalus, with Cordoba as its center). The rapid assimilation and subsequent enrichment of Greek philosophy and science, along with contacts with Indian astronomy and mathematics, were fueled by caliphate funding.

The section on scholars and centers of knowledge emphasizes the translation movement from Greek and Syriac as the first phase. Hunayn ibn Ishaq (808 - after 856 CE) is highlighted for his role in inventing scholarly and philosophical vocabulary and verifying texts, demonstrating that new problems arose as societies developed, leading to new knowledge. In 832 CE, the House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma) was founded in Baghdad by Caliph Al-Ma’mûn. This institution, inspired by Hellenistic rationalist philosophy, served not only as a library and translation center but also as a research hub with a hospital and astronomical observatory. Historian A. Koyré noted that Arabs were not just intermediaries but "masters and educators" of the Latin West, possessing genuine philosophical understanding. Baghdad became the center of knowledge from the 7th to 9th centuries, with knowledge centers diversifying and competing across the expanding empire in the 10th and 11th centuries.

The House of Wisdom benefited from caliphate patronage to boost prestige. Its activities expanded significantly under al-Ma'mûn, becoming an academy, translation center, and research institution. The widespread adoption of paper (a Chinese invention, whose secret was obtained after the Battle of Talas in 751 CE) greatly simplified and reduced the cost of book dissemination, creating a new book market. Baghdad fostered a diverse intellectual environment where scholars of different cultures and faiths worked together based solely on scientific competence, making Arabic a scientific language in its own right.

An example of the classification of sciences by Al-Fârâbî (872-950 CE) is provided, categorizing knowledge into: Language Sciences, Logic, Mathematics (including arithmetic, geometry, optics, astronomy, music, weights, industrial techniques), Physics (natural science, including meteorology, mineralogy, plants, animals, humans), Theology, and Political Science.

Astronomy was particularly important for practical reasons: determining the start of Ramadan, the direction of Mecca for prayers, and prayer times. Its theoretical framework combined Hellenistic science (Ptolemy's Almagest), Aristotelian concepts, and Indian contributions (decimal numeration). Arab scholars greatly improved observations and instruments like the astrolabe, which allowed them to measure star heights, time, and direction, and served to verify Ptolemy's results. These astronomical advances stimulated the development of mathematics (decimal numeration, trigonometry) and optics, benefiting navigation. The importance of astrology and horoscopes is also mentioned.

In sciences of calculation, Al-Khwarizmi (9th century, Baghdad) is a key figure. His treatise "On the Art of Counting of the Indians" introduced the decimal system, including zero as a digit, and basic operations for practical problems, though still using rhetorical rather than symbolic algebra. His "Compendium on Calculation by Restoration and Balancing" (al-jabr wa al-muqabala) is considered the first algebra textbook, systematically studying quadratic equations and providing algorithms for their solution. "Al-jabr" (completion) involved moving terms to ensure positive coefficients, and "al-muqabala" (balancing) meant reducing terms on both sides. His work included geometric proofs for algorithms, accepting only positive roots.

In Geography, Muhammad al-Idrisi (1100-1166 CE) created the Tabula Rogeriana (1154 CE) for King Roger II of Sicily. This map included regional maps and a projection of the known world (Eurasia and much of Africa), with detailed data on climate, politics, and culture. It remained among the most precise maps for centuries, notably depicting the South at the top.

Alchemy (Al-kīmiyā) in the Islamic world had both recipe-based and spiritual objectives. Originating in Alexandria (early CE) with Egyptian, Eastern (Chinese Taoism), Neoplatonic, and Christian Gnostic influences, it was rooted in the hermetic tradition of secrecy and initiation. Alchemical practice aimed to reproduce natural transformations by finding processes to shift qualities, with the ultimate goal of transmuting base metals into gold, seen as both a material and spiritual purification. It involved correspondences between the microcosm and macrocosm (7 metals linked to 7 planets) and various operations (sublimation, distillation, etc.), leading to the development of numerous techniques (calcination, alambic, alcohol, nitric acid). The theory posited four elements plus two principles (sulfur and mercury), acting on qualities to modify bodies, assuming nature originated from a single substance.

Jabir ibn Hayyam (Geber) (~721 - ~815 CE) linked the sciences of nature to the science of revelation, believing that a journey from the exoteric (zahir) to the esoteric (batin) was needed to understand nature as revealing the spiritual world. Al-Razi (Rhazès), however, rejected prophecy and this esoteric interpretation, transforming Geber's alchemy into chemistry by studying natural facts as facts, not symbols. Rhazès and Avicenna notably denied the possibility of transmutation.

The chapter concludes by noting that the extension of the empire in the 10th and 11th centuries led to the dispersal and competition of knowledge centers. Baghdad's role as a primary intellectual hub declined, and it was eventually destroyed by the Mongols in 1258. This contrasts with Western Europe, which had lost most ancient scientific knowledge due to wars, epidemics, and poverty. The Ottoman takeover in the 14th-15th centuries further froze cultural dynamism by, for example, refusing printing paper.
