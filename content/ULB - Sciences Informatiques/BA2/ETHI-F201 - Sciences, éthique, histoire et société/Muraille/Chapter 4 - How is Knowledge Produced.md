Chapter 4, "Comment la connaissance est-elle produite ?" (How is knowledge produced?), delves into the evolution of scientific methodology, exploring rationalism, empiricism, experimental methods, and statistical analysis, as well as the challenges posed by the complexity of living systems and the human sciences. It also addresses the social norms of science and the critical issue of distinguishing science from pseudoscience and forming scientific consensus.

**4.1 Evolution of Scientific Methodology:** This section begins with rationalism, which posits reason as the sole source of certain knowledge, exemplified by Euclidean geometry where theorems are deduced from evident axioms. Key figures include Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz. However, rationalism can be dogmatic and prone to ideological bias, producing formal statements that don't directly describe the external world.

The principle of parsimony (Ockham's Razor), advocating for the simplest sufficient hypotheses, is also introduced. The principle of linear causality, asserting that every effect has an identifiable cause, emerged in the 17th-18th centuries.

Next, empiricism is discussed, opposing rationalism by asserting that all knowledge comes from sensory experiences, which are then abstracted into general concepts. Empiricism is antidogmatic, acknowledging the impossibility of absolute truths and highlighting the "problem of induction" (Hume's black swan).
- Pioneers like Roger Bacon, Francis Bacon (who introduced the method of induction, moving from particular observations to general theories), and Thomas Hobbes are highlighted.
- Isaac Newton's physics, based on rigorous observation, further cemented the victory of empiricism over Cartesian rationalism.
- John Stuart Mill formalized inductive logic, combining observation with logical principles to establish general laws.

The experimental method, particularly the hypothetico-deductive approach, is presented as a synthesis of empiricism and rationalism.
- Claude Bernard, in 1865, formalized this method, where observations lead to theories, theories generate testable hypotheses, and experiments validate or invalidate these theories, making experimentation an arbiter in scientific theory selection. This approach aims for mechanistic explanations, elucidating causal chains.
- Charles Sanders Peirce's "scientific inquiry" further elaborated on combining observation, abduction (seeking the simplest explanation), deduction, and inductive tests, emphasizing the role of the scientific community. The distinction between causality and correlation is crucial; experimentation is necessary to identify causal chains, not just statistical correlations.

Experimental models, being simplified and controlled representations of reality, are essential for identifying causality but can also incorporate researcher biases. The rise of statistical analysis (pioneered by Galton, Fisher, Pearson) integrated uncertainty into scientific results, acknowledging that even rigorous work doesn't lead to absolute certainties.

**4.2 The Problem of the Living (or Complexity):** This section addresses the unique challenges of studying complex living organisms. Vitalism, an older concept, posited a "vital principle" in living beings distinct from physicochemical properties. However, methodological reductionism (mechanistic/analytic approach), inspired by Descartes and promoted by neo-positivism, largely supplanted it.

Reductionism aims to understand complex systems by breaking them down into simpler components, often by reducing parameters and elements in experimental models. While effective for controlled studies, reductionism has theoretical limits (e.g., assuming linearity, destroying the object of study) and practical limits (e.g., findings in simplified models may not translate to real-world complexity).

In contrast, the systemic (or holistic) approach, formalized by Ludwig Von Bertalanffy, studies systems in their globality, emphasizing dynamic interactions where the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. This approach explains emergent phenomena but is difficult to implement due to the sheer volume of data and interactions. In biology, a complementarity between reductionist and systemic approaches is often sought, leading to a mixed epistemology and pluralistic ontology, implying no single "universal scientific method".

**4.3 Social Norms of Science (Ethos of Science):** Modern science is a collective endeavor governed by social norms. Robert K. Merton, the father of the sociology of science, identified four institutional imperatives forming the scientific "ethos" in 1942:
1. communalism (sharing resources and results)
2. universalism (rejection of cultural, ethnic, religious, ideological biases)
3. organized skepticism (peer review and critical evaluation)
4. disinterestedness (researchers not seeking personal profit).

These norms aim to foster cooperation and reduce biases, emphasizing that scientific claims should be judged on their merits, not the personal attributes of the proponent. Michael Polanyi and André F. Cournand further stressed the importance of scientific autonomy, methodological rigor, integrity, and intellectual freedom.

The modern scientific methodology actively attempts to neutralize social biases through peer review before publication and reproducibility of observations by independent researchers and in different models.

**4.4 Epistemology of Human Sciences:** Human sciences (SH), like biology, grapple with complexity but face specific challenges: the difficulty of experimentation or technological development prevents effective theory refutation, leading to a plurality of competing theories. Additionally, SH theories can influence reality (reflexivity), as society changes as it is studied.

While sociology was founded on positivist epistemology by Auguste Comte and Émile Durkheim, it has been heavily influenced by constructivism since the 1970s-80s. This constructivist approach in SH rejects the idea of a pre-existing objective reality in favor of social construction, emphasizing "situated knowledge" and the importance of the researcher's social position (e.g., class, gender, race) in achieving "greater objectivity".

Some researchers advocate for "epistemic privilege" for marginalized groups and the inclusion of personal experience and local knowledge. This approach legitimizes political activism in research, abandoning the ideal of "value-free science" for social justice. Critics argue that accepting non-epistemic values makes scientific knowledge no longer neutral or objective, and it's unclear how to reconcile contradictory positions or feelings.

**4.5 Demarcation between Science and Pseudoscience:** The problem of demarcation, central to realism, is to differentiate true, justified beliefs ("scientific knowledge") from mere beliefs. Historically, scientific knowledge was characterized by certainty and evidence, as per Descartes. However, the 20th-century "fallibilism" challenged this, arguing that all current theories are likely false given the history of scientific replacements.

Various criteria have been proposed for demarcation:
- **Inductivism (Logical Empiricism):** A scientific theory is built logically from verifiable observations. Critiques include that many true scientific theories weren't developed purely by induction (e.g., Galileo's thought experiments). The "problem of induction" (Hume, Russell's turkey) highlights the impossibility of certainty from finite observations.
	- The Duhem-Quine thesis suggests empirical data alone can't uniquely determine the "best" theory among alternatives.
	- The Sellars-Hanson thesis argues that observation is theory-laden, meaning theories influence what is observed and how data is interpreted. Kuhn further noted that social context influences which theory is preferred, suggesting a "blind change" not necessarily leading to progress.
- **Verificationism:** An assertion only has cognitive meaning if it's verifiable by experience, rejecting metaphysics. However, verification isn't sufficient as "miracles" can happen (e.g., Pasteur's vaccine success despite a false initial hypothesis).
- **Falsificationism (Karl Popper):** A theory's scientific character is tied to its potential for experimental refutation; if it can be contradicted by possible observations, it's scientific. This implies clarity, precision, and that the method of discovery doesn't matter. Irrefutable claims (like Russell's Teapot) are not scientific.
	- Popper believed that corroborated (unrefuted) theories are approximations of truth, hierarchizing scientific theories. However, falsification has flaws: some scientific disciplines can't make precise predictions or reproducible experiments, and judging a theory's falsehood from inconclusive experiments is complex due to observation fallibility and underdetermination. In practice, researchers might question the experiment or postulate new entities to reconcile theory and observation.

Many 21st-century authors now advocate for multi-criteria approaches to demarcation, acknowledging that science isn't unified and criteria vary by discipline (e.g., empirical adequacy, reproducibility, refutability, simplicity). The main criterion for modern science is the possibility of confrontation with reality, not just reason.

**4.6 The Production of Scientific Consensus:** Scientific consensus is an agreement within a discipline about a description, causal chain, or theory considered "true" in the sense of conforming to reality. Achieving scientific consensus requires believing in a single, knowable reality independent of mental representations, governed by constant natural laws, and rejecting supernatural causes (i.e., adherence to realism, materialism, naturalism).

While natural sciences often achieve "liquid" consensus (slowly generated by accumulating evidence), human sciences often lack it due to multiple ontologies and epistemologies. Medicine, however, demands rapid consensus for quality care and mass treatments, leading to its pioneering role in rigorous validation methods.

The section illustrates this with the historical example of George Washington's death from bleeding and the Rush versus Cobbett trial, showing how personal experience and even court decisions historically validated medical practices.

The shift towards Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) after 1962, following drug tragedies and the need for rigorous testing, marked a revolution in public health. EBM, pioneered by Archibald Cochrane and Gordon H. Guyatt, relies on systematic reviews of controlled clinical trials, prioritizing evidence over expert opinion or intuition.

Its three main principles are:
1. hierarchy of evidence (controlled observations over uncontrolled)
2. totality of evidence (evaluating all available evidence)
3. contextualization (considering patient values and resources).

EBM is a realist epistemology, using confrontation with reality to determine treatment efficacy, focusing on observational evidence and data collection, and constantly re-evaluating treatments. Critiques of EBM include its perceived reductionism, discouragement of deliberation, and potential conflicts of interest from industry-funded trials. The rebellion against EBM, exemplified by Didier Raoult's postmodern scientific stance, highlights the conflict between scientific norms and the constructivist view that scientific consensus is a product of power relations and biases.
