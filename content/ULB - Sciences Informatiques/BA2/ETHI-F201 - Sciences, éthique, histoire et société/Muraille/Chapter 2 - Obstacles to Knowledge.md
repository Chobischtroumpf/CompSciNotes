Chapter 2, "Les obstacles à la connaissance" (Obstacles to knowledge), highlights that humans are not naturally equipped to produce scientific knowledge due to numerous cognitive biases, necessitating the use of specific methods, primarily philosophy and science.

It argues that scientific knowledge often contradicts "common sense" and sensory perceptions, citing historical examples like the Earth being flat or the Sun revolving around the Earth.

The chapter details various cognitive biases, including perception biases (like the Adelson checkerboard illusion) and judgment biases (such as anchoring and confirmation bias). It also introduces Francis Bacon's "idols" as ancestors to the concept of bias.

The Dunning-Kruger effect and "cultural cognition thesis" are discussed, explaining how individuals may overestimate their competence and align factual beliefs with group identity. The chapter concludes that relying on "popular common sense" or senses leads to ignorance, underscoring the need for rigorous methods to access knowledge of reality, bridging philosophy and science.
